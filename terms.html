What the product does, and what it does not

VisionGuard is camera software that runs on a computer at the customer's own site. It watches the cameras for the features the customer switches on, such as attendance by face, people counting, restricted zones, track a person, knife detection, lost bag and the vehicle gate. Most of these keep a record of what they find.

It is a tool that helps people notice things and decide. It does not decide for them, and it takes no physical action: it does not lock or open a door or gate, call anyone or stop anyone. Apart from keeping its own records, it does two things by itself. It sends the alert emails an administrator has switched on. And the object-detection library it is built on, Ultralytics, sends usage statistics to Google Analytics while the computer is connected to the internet. These include the model file's name, its speed, the computer's processor and graphics card, and a fixed code for the computer, but never pictures, names or site records. This is that library's default setting, and VisionGuard does not switch it off today.

Its limits today:

  • Any detection can be wrong. Treat every alert, count, name and plate as something for a person to check.
  • Knife detection: a possible knife shows on screen at once. If knife emails are switched on, an email also goes out at once for each new incident, marked as not yet confirmed by a person. The entry stays unconfirmed until a person confirms it or marks it a false alarm; if emails are on, a second email then follows. False alarms happen, and they stay in the history. How well it catches a real knife has not been measured yet. Guns are not detected.
  • People counting over-counts today. In our recorded tests, one person alone was read as anywhere from one to seven, about two and a half on average, and an empty room showed people who were not there on about three readings in four. Totals across cameras are added together, so a person seen by two cameras can be counted twice.
  • Face recognition can name the wrong person, or miss someone it should know. A name on screen is the system's best match, not proof of who someone is. Its accuracy on a real group of enrolled people has not been measured yet.
  • Number-plate reading has not yet been measured on real plates at a site, so a plate can be misread.
  • Ask, which answers typed questions, can misunderstand a question. It opens the page for the search it chose, with the person, place or date it picked already filled in, so check those before you rely on the result. When Ask is set up with a Gemini key, it sends the question, together with the site's area and group names, to Google's Gemini. It first replaces the names of enrolled people, and the plates on the site's vehicle register, with placeholders; any other word, which can include a name, is sent as typed. If the Gemini account is on Google's free tier, Google's terms let it use what is sent and let people at Google read it.
  • Lost bag outlines bags of a chosen colour only while someone is watching that camera. It keeps no record and sends no alert.

Decisions that affect someone's safety, job or legal position should always be made by a person, using what VisionGuard shows as one input, not the final word.
